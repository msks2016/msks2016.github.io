## Significance

Long non-coding RNAs are best known from cancer and developmental biology, but they also shape traits that matter for food: they help regulate fruit ripening and stress tolerance in crops, and muscle growth and milk production in livestock. Foods themselves carry RNA. Milk, for example, contains extracellular vesicles loaded with RNA, including lncRNAs, which raises the questions of whether dietary RNAs survive processing and digestion, and whether they have any effect in the person who eats them. At the same time, experimental studies report that bioactive compounds in the diet can change the expression of human lncRNAs linked to cancer and metabolic disease. These findings are spread across plant science, animal science, nutrition and cancer biology, and a review that brings them together can help food scientists and RNA biologists see where the evidence is solid and where it is still speculative.

## Project summary

This review, now in preparation, is organised around three questions. First, how do lncRNAs regulate traits that determine the yield, quality and safety of plant and animal foods? Second, which lncRNAs are present in foods such as milk, how stable are they during processing and digestion, and what evidence is there for effects in consumers? Third, how do dietary components and bioactive compounds alter lncRNA expression in the human body, particularly in relation to cancer? For each question, the review will summarise the main findings, weigh the strength of the evidence and the methods used to detect lncRNAs in complex food samples, and set out open questions and opportunities for future research.
