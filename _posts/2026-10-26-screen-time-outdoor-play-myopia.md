Myopia, or short-sightedness, is becoming one of the most common eye conditions in the world: by 2050 it is projected to affect nearly five billion people. Long hours of close-up work, including time on phones, tablets and computers, are thought to be one driver, while time outdoors appears to protect children's eyes. The COVID-19 pandemic, when schools closed and children spent far more time at home and on screens, created an unplanned test of these ideas.

## What we did

In this study, published in the *Journal of Biosciences and Public Health*, we studied 390 school-going children aged 5–12 years with myopia who attended a specialised eye hospital in Dhaka, Bangladesh, between January 2020 and December 2022. We reviewed two years of clinical records, including visual acuity and cycloplegic refraction, and interviewed parents using a structured questionnaire with 24-hour recalls of their child's screen time. We then compared three periods: before, during and after the pandemic restrictions.

## What we found

- During lockdowns, children's average daily screen time rose from about 2.1 to 5.6 hours.
- Over two years, average refractive error worsened from −2.4 to −4.6 dioptres.
- After restrictions ended, screen time fell to about 2.7 hours a day, and the share of children taking part in outdoor activities rose from roughly one in five to about seven in ten.
- As screen time fell and outdoor activity returned, myopia progression slowed noticeably.

## Why it matters

Home confinement was associated with a sharp acceleration of myopia, and the return to less screen time and more outdoor activity was followed by stabilisation. Because this was an observational study in a single hospital, it cannot prove cause and effect, but the pattern fits a growing body of research. The practical message for parents and schools is simple: balance device use with regular time outdoors.
