Adolescence is a period of rapid growth, and nutrition during these years shapes health for decades. In rural Bangladesh, school-going adolescents are growing up with traditional home-cooked meals alongside packaged and fast food, and both undernutrition and overweight are concerns.

## What we did

We carried out a cross-sectional survey of 378 school-going adolescents in rural areas of Bangladesh, collecting information on family background, meal patterns and how often they ate different foods, and assessing stunting and body-mass index (BMI). The results were published in *Current Research in Nutrition and Food Science* (2024).

## What we found

- About two-thirds (64.3%) followed the traditional pattern of three meals a day, and 26% ate fast food regularly.
- Many diets were missing key food groups: 23.0% did not eat fruit at least once a day, 19.6% did not eat chicken or fish, and 6.0% did not eat vegetables.
- 36.0% of the adolescents were stunted, including 2.9% who were severely stunted, while 37.3% were overweight according to BMI.
- The severity of stunting was linked to BMI category, gender and parents' monthly income. BMI category also differed by gender, but not by age.

## Why it matters

When stunting and overweight are both common in the same population, a pattern often called the double burden of malnutrition, policies need to tackle both at once. School-based programmes that encourage regular, balanced meals with fruit, vegetables and protein, and that take family income into account, are a practical place to start.
