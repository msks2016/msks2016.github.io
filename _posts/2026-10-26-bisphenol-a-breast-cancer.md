Bisphenol A (BPA) is used to make polycarbonate plastics and the epoxy resins that line many food and drink cans. Because it can mimic the hormone estrogen, researchers have long asked whether everyday exposure might influence hormone-sensitive cancers such as breast cancer. Data from Bangladesh, where plastic use has grown quickly, have been scarce.

## What we did

In this cross-sectional study, published in the *Journal of Environment Pollution and Human Health* (2024), we recruited 55 women with breast cancer at a cancer hospital in Dhaka between September 2022 and February 2023, together with five women without cancer as a comparison group. We combined interviews and clinical records with blood samples, and measured serum BPA using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA).

## What we found

- Average serum BPA was about 4.0 ng/mL in the women without cancer, compared with roughly 17–34 ng/mL across age groups of patients: four to seven times higher.
- Among patients, higher BPA levels went together with more severe symptoms, such as breast tenderness and physical weakness.
- Most of the patients were married women living in urban areas, and more than half were in paid employment.

## What it can and cannot tell us

A cross-sectional study with a small comparison group cannot show that BPA causes breast cancer. Women with cancer may differ from the comparison group in many other ways, and BPA levels measured after diagnosis could also reflect treatment or changes in daily life. What the study does show is that BPA exposure is measurable, and potentially high, in this population, which makes the case for larger case–control and cohort studies. In the meantime, reducing unnecessary contact with plastics, such as avoiding heating food in plastic containers, is a sensible precaution.
