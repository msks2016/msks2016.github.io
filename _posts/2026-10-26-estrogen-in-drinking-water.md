Endocrine-disrupting chemicals can interfere with the body's hormone systems even at very low concentrations. One of the most potent is 17α-ethinylestradiol (EE2), the synthetic estrogen used in oral contraceptives, which can reach rivers and reservoirs through wastewater. Intensive agriculture and poor waste disposal add to the concern in fast-growing cities. We wanted to know how much EE2 ends up in the water people drink in one of Dhaka's most densely populated areas.

## What we did

We sampled raw and treated water at a major water treatment plant in 2023 and 2024, together with household tap water, bottled mineral water, filtered water, water from a household purification unit, and the large refillable "jar" water that is widely sold in the city. EE2 was measured using a quantitative enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). The study was published in the *American Journal of Water Resources* (2024).

## What we found

- Where EE2 could be measured, concentrations ranged from about 3 to 20 ng/L.
- Jar water had the highest level, about 20 ng/L.
- In filtered and bottled water, levels were mostly below 0.01 ng/L, too low to measure reliably.
- Levels varied somewhat between locations and sampling times, without a consistent pattern.

## Why it matters

Jar water is a cheap and widely used source of drinking water in Dhaka, so its quality deserves closer monitoring. ELISA is a practical screening tool, but immunoassays can also react with related compounds. Confirming these results with chromatography–mass spectrometry, and linking water levels to measures of human exposure, would be useful next steps.
